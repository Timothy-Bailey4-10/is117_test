# Selling a White T-Shirt

### A short field guide to persuasion, meaning, and design — and to directing AI with all three

---

This is a mini textbook about how ordinary things are given meaning.

It uses one product for the entire book: **a plain white cotton T-shirt.** No logo, no pattern, no story. By the end, that same unchanged shirt will have been sold five different ways, to five different people, at prices from $34 to $340 — without a single thread being altered.

The shirt is a device. The real subject is the machinery that makes the difference: how communication influences decisions, how brands attach identity to objects, and how visual language carries beliefs. The final chapter turns that machinery around and points it at something you will use immediately — **how to specify work precisely enough that an AI can do it and you can tell whether it did.**

## Chapters

### [1. Persuasion and Human Decision-Making](01-persuasion.md)
How communication shapes attention, interpretation, trust, and action. Cialdini's principles of influence, the difference between persuasion and manipulation, and framing.
**Asks:** *What response are we trying to enable?*

### [2. Brand Archetypes and Meaning](02-archetypes.md)
How brands attach identity to products using familiar story patterns. Twelve working archetypes, three versions of the same shirt, and why archetypes are models rather than diagnoses.
**Asks:** *What meaning or identity are we expressing?*

### [3. Modernism, Postmodernism, and Visual Language](03-design-language.md)
How design traditions carry arguments. Bauhaus, Swiss Style, the postmodern reaction, and the tensions still visible in today's interfaces. Includes a method for reading any design and a guide to researching real objects in museum collections.
**Asks:** *How should that meaning look and feel?*

### [4. The Plain White T-Shirt Case Study](04-white-tshirt-case-study.md)
All three lenses at once. Five complete brand concepts for one identical shirt — audience, archetype, persuasion, visual language, headline, story, and the specific ethical risk each one carries.
**Asks:** *What happens when you actually run the machinery?*

### [5. Synthesis — Three Lenses and a Pit Stop](05-synthesis.md)
Why these three questions work as a specification language for AI-assisted work. Bounded tasks, deterministic checks versus probabilistic review, why Git matters more when a machine is writing, and what stays human.
**Asks:** *Who is responsible?*

## How to Use This Guide

**Read it in order.** Each chapter adds a question, and Chapter 4 only works once you have all three. Chapter 5 depends on Chapter 4.

**Track the shirt.** It appears in every chapter. When you meet it, stop and ask what changed — it is never the product.

**Do the exercises.** Chapter 1 ends with ethical questions, Chapter 2 with eight positioning questions, Chapter 3 with a method and a museum research assignment, Chapter 5 with seven questions for discussion. Reading them is not the same as answering them.

**Verify the research yourself.** Chapter 3 gives you museum search terms, not citations. That is deliberate. Find the real objects, read the real catalog entries, and notice where they complicate the tidy version told here.

**Treat the examples as hypothetical.** Every brand, headline, and price in this book is invented for teaching. No real company is being described, and none of it should be repeated as fact.

**Argue with it.** Chapter 3 is a chapter about a century-long disagreement over what good design is. It would be strange for a book on that subject to expect agreement.

## About This Book

Each chapter was built through a bounded workflow: a GitHub issue defining the task, a dedicated branch, an AI assistant working to a written specification, human review of the diff, a commit referencing the issue, and an automated check on merge.

That process is also the subject of Chapter 5. The book is an example of the thing it describes.
